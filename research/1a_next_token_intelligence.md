# Part 1a — Why "just predicting the next token" can produce genuine competence

Research notes for the video series (English; synthesis into Swedish happens later).
Date of research: 15 September 2026. **Revised after red-team review (same day)** — edits are marked
"[corrected after red team]". Items marked **[verify]** should be double-checked before they go on camera.
See "RESPONSE TO RED TEAM" at the end for the verdict-by-verdict table.

Framing note for the creator: the audience's objection is "it only predicts the next word, so it
can't really be intelligent". The strongest response is not to argue about the word "really". It is
a three-step move: (1) predicting text *well* forces a model to build internal models of the world
that produced the text (items 1–8); (2) since ~2024 the models are no longer *only* next-token
predictors — they are trained with reinforcement learning to solve problems, and they now do things
no human has done (items 9–14, 22); (3) whether this counts as "real" understanding is a philosophy
question that does not matter for risk, because competence is what kills you, not consciousness
(items 15–21, 23). Every item has an honest "what a skeptic will say" section; use them.

Three rules the red team and I agree on: never say "conscious"; never say "the experts agree it
understands"; lead each segment with a non-lab source (IMO, METR, Tao/Gowers/Alon, Hugging Face's
own staff, Mitchell) and include the scandals (Oct 2025 Erdős over-claim, GPT-5.6 Sol cheating).

## A. Why prediction forces understanding

### 1. The murder-mystery test (Sutskever)
- **Core claim (as you'd say it in a video):** Imagine feeding a model a 300-page detective novel, and the last sentence is "The murderer is ___". To predict that one word well, the model cannot just know grammar or which words often follow which. It has to have tracked every character, every alibi, every clue. "Predicting the next word" describes the *exam*, not the *student*. The exam is easy to state and arbitrarily hard to pass. Whether today's models pass it *well* is an empirical question — which is what the rest of the video is about.
- **Best formulation / quote:** Ilya Sutskever (then OpenAI chief scientist), fireside chat with Jensen Huang at NVIDIA GTC, March 2023 (NVIDIA published it 22 March 2023 [corrected after red team: earlier draft said 15 March; GTC ran 20–23 March]): "Say you read a detective novel... at the last page the detective says, 'I'm going to reveal the identity of whoever committed the crime and that person's name is'. Predict that word." And: "When we train a large neural network to accurately predict the next word in lots of different texts from the Internet, what we are doing is that we are learning a world model... What the neural network learns is some representation of the process that produced the text." To Dwarkesh Patel (27 March 2023): "Predicting the next token well means that you understand the underlying reality that led to the creation of that token."
- **Who has explained this well (links):**
  - Sutskever–Huang fireside chat, GTC 2023 (video): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GI4Tpi48DlA [verify exact URL]; transcript excerpts: https://lifearchitect.ai/ilya/
  - Dwarkesh Patel interview with Sutskever, 27 Mar 2023: https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/ilya-sutskever
  - Andrej Karpathy, "Intro to Large Language Models" (Nov 2023), first ~20 min: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zjkBMFhNj_g
  - 3Blue1Brown, "But what is a GPT?" (2024): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wjZofJX0v4M
- **Evidence:** The analogy has been turned into a benchmark: "WhoDunIt" (Feb 2025, arXiv 2502.07747) tests culprit detection in mystery stories and credits the Sutskever–Huang exchange. https://arxiv.org/html/2502.07747
- **Strengths for a lay audience:** Everyone has read a detective story; the point lands in ten seconds.
- **Weaknesses / what a skeptic will say:** [corrected after red team] Bender & Koller's "octopus" objection: the argument shows what *perfect* prediction would require, not what *actual* models achieve — a model can guess "the murderer is [most common culprit in similar novels]" and be right often enough. Also: Sutskever is an interested party, and he himself said in Nov 2025 that models "generalize dramatically worse than people" (item 19). Rebuttal: concede both immediately and hand over to items 3–6 (what is actually inside) and 10–14 (what they actually do). Quote Sutskever's 2025 caveat yourself to look fair.
- **Caveats / accuracy notes:** Present as an argument, not an authority claim. Date as above.
- **Score: 9/10** — simplest, most memorable, hardest to dismiss; the natural opener.

### 2. Compression is understanding (the JPEG, flipped) — 30-second supplement to item 1
- **[corrected after red team: demoted from standalone item to a supplement; rebuilt around Ted Chiang's metaphor]**
- **Core claim:** Critics call a language model "a blurry JPEG of the web" (Ted Chiang, New Yorker, Feb 2023) — and that is fair. But you cannot squeeze the internet a hundredfold by *remembering* it; you have to learn the rules that generated it. A JPEG doesn't know what a face is. A thing that must redraw a million faces from a tiny description effectively does. Mathematically the training objective *is* compression, and the shortest description of a billion pages is a description of the world that produced them.
- **Best formulation / quote:** The Hutter Prize (Marcus Hutter, 2006; 500,000 EUR pool since 2020; compress 1 GB of Wikipedia) states its rationale: "compressing natural language text is a hard AI problem, equivalent to passing the Turing test." Karpathy (Intro to LLMs, 2023): the weights are "a lossy compression of the internet." Sutskever's Simons Institute talk (Aug 2023) formalises it: gradient descent searches for the shortest "program" that explains the data.
- **Links:** Chiang, "ChatGPT Is a Blurry JPEG of the Web" (New Yorker, 9 Feb 2023): https://www.newyorker.com/tech/annals-of-technology/chatgpt-is-a-blurry-jpeg-of-the-web ; Sutskever talk https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AKMuA_TVz3A ; notes https://sumanthrh.com/post/notes-on-generalization/ ; Hutter Prize https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutter_Prize
- **Evidence (footnotes only):** 2024 Hutter Prize record (Orav & Knoll, fx2-cmix): 1 GB → ~110.8 MB using neural predictors. Rofin, Naghiyev & Hahn (Mar 2026, arXiv 2603.14087): next-token training itself makes transformers build abstract features that are "seemingly useless" for the next token but predictive of tokens far ahead, especially in code — abstract verified by me. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14087
- **Strengths:** Pre-empts the skeptic's best metaphor by conceding it first, then flipping it.
- **Weaknesses / skeptic:** "A small lookup table is also compressed"; "hallucinations are the JPEG artefacts"; the Hutter Prize is a hypothesis and its winners are not AGIs; Solomonoff talk sounds mystical. Rebuttal: don't argue theory — point to items 3–6 where the "rules" are found inside the model.
- **Caveats:** Don't say the model is "literally the shortest program". Keep the Kolmogorov material off-camera.
- **Score: 6/10 standalone; 8/10 as the 30-second flip on Chiang.**

### 3. Othello-GPT and Chess-GPT: the board that was never shown
- **Core claim:** Researchers trained a small GPT only on *sequences of moves* from the board game Othello — strings like "E3 D3 C4" — with no board, no rules, no pictures. Then they looked inside. The network had built an internal 8×8 map of the board, updated move by move, which it used to play legal moves. When they surgically edited that internal map, the model's next move changed accordingly. The same has been shown for chess: a 50-million-parameter model trained only on text move lists plays at ~1300 Elo (a decent club player) and contains a linear map of the board. "Predict the next token" produced a *model of the game*.
- **Best formulation / quote:** Kenneth Li et al. (Harvard, ICLR 2023 oral): the model "learns to compute the state of the board at each move" despite never being given it. Neel Nanda (DeepMind) showed the representation is *linear* and encoded as "mine / theirs" rather than black/white — the model invented its own bookkeeping. Karvonen (2024): Chess-GPT was "never explicitly given the state of the board or the rules of chess." [corrected after red team — say the critic's line yourself:] Melanie Mitchell likens the internal representation to Ptolemy's epicycles "rather than a modern orrery". Reply: epicycles predicted the planets for 1,400 years. A messy internal model that *works* and was *never programmed* is exactly the point.
- **Links:** Li et al. https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13382 ; Nanda https://www.neelnanda.io/mechanistic-interpretability/othello , https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00941 ; Karvonen https://adamkarvonen.github.io/machine_learning/2024/01/03/chess-world-models.html , https://adamkarvonen.github.io/machine_learning/2024/03/20/chess-gpt-interventions.html , arXiv 2403.15498 ; Mitchell (skeptical review) https://aiguide.substack.com/p/llms-and-world-models-part-2
- **Evidence:** Nanda's "mine/yours/empty" linear probe reaches 99.5% board-state accuracy (layer 7), and the representation is *causal* (editing it changes legal-move predictions). Karvonen's chess probes also recover player skill as a latent variable. Tehenan et al. (June 2025, arXiv 2506.02996; abstract verified): linear spatial world models in general-purpose LLMs, confirmed by causal interventions. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02996
- **B-roll (folded in from former item 4) [corrected after red team]:** Gurnee & Tegmark (Oct 2023, ICLR 2024): Llama-2 keeps a linear internal *map* — latitude/longitude of cities and a timeline of historical figures are readable from activations, with "space neurons" and "time neurons". Mostly correlational; show the recovered world map on screen, say "map-like representation", don't say "knows where things are like you do". https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02207
- **Strengths for a lay audience:** A controlled experiment with an unambiguous, showable result (a board appearing inside the network). Directly refutes "it only knows which words follow which".
- **Weaknesses / what a skeptic will say:** The "bag of heuristics" follow-up (students in a DeepMind training programme led by Nanda, mid-2024, **blog post, not peer-reviewed** [corrected after red team]): Othello-GPT "computes whether cells are 'mine' or 'yours' by aggregating many heuristic rules, rather than implementing a crisp algorithm." Mitchell: "The claims of emergent abstract world models in LLMs are not yet supported by strong evidence." Probes can find structure a model doesn't use (probe ≠ mechanism). Toy models (millions, not trillions, of parameters).
- **Caveats:** Keep "small model" on screen and hand off to item 5 for frontier scale. Don't say "clean, complete model".
- **Score: 8/10** [corrected after red team: 9→8 for the toy-scale and epicycles caveats] — the most convincing *demonstration*; visual, controlled, honest about limits.

### 5. Claude plans its rhymes (Anthropic, "Tracing the thoughts", March 2025) + Golden Gate Claude
- **Core claim:** The folk picture is that a model writes word by word with no plan. Anthropic built a "microscope" for Claude 3.5 Haiku and found the opposite. [corrected after red team: lead with the inconvenient finding to build trust] The same microscope caught Claude *making up* its explanation of how it added 36+59 (it describes "carrying the 1"; internally it runs two parallel paths, one for rough magnitude and one for the last digit). And it also caught Claude choosing the rhyme word *before* writing the line: asked for a couplet ending in "grab it", it activates "rabbit" ahead of time, then writes a line to land on it. Suppress "rabbit" and it plans "habit"; inject "green" and it writes towards green. It answers "capital of the state containing Dallas" via an internal step Dallas→Texas→Austin — swap the Texas feature for California and it says Sacramento. And it represents "small/opposite/large" in a language-independent space shared across English, French and Chinese.
- **Best quotes:** "Before starting the second line, it began 'thinking' of potential on-topic words that would rhyme with 'grab it'. Then, with these plans in mind, it writes a line to end with the planned word." "A shared abstract space where meanings exist and where thinking can happen before being translated into specific languages." "Language models like Claude aren't programmed directly by humans — instead, they're trained on large amounts of data. During that training process, they learn their own strategies to solve problems."
- **Golden Gate Claude (the 60-second demo):** In May 2024 Anthropic found ~34 million interpretable "features" inside Claude 3 Sonnet; the Golden Gate Bridge feature fires on the bridge in English, in Japanese text, and in photos. They turned it up and released the model for 24 hours: asked how to spend $10, it suggested paying the bridge toll. Proof-by-demo that the internals are organised by *meaning*, and that a concept can be located and manipulated. https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/
- **Links:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/tracing-thoughts-language-model ; full paper https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/biology.html ; MIT Tech Review https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/03/27/1113916/anthropic-can-now-track-the-bizarre-inner-workings-of-a-large-language-model ; method updates continue (Circuits Updates May/June 2026: https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/may-update/index.html , https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/june-update/index.html )
- **Strengths:** "It plans the rhyme before it writes the line" kills the autocomplete picture in one sentence; causal manipulation, published method, replicated with open tools.
- **Weaknesses / skeptic:** "Anthropic has a commercial interest" (true; the inconvenient findings were published too); "planning one word ahead is not planning a war" (correct — don't oversell); "features are researcher-imposed labels" (the steering result is behavioural, not a label); feature imprecision documented in 2026 follow-ups (e.g. "When the coffee feature activates on coffins", arXiv 2601.03047 — [verify] before citing).
- **Caveats (must not overclaim):** Anthropic's own words: "Even on short, simple prompts, our method only captures a fraction of the total computation performed by Claude, and the mechanisms we do see may have some artifacts." Say "we can trace a small fraction, and what we see is structured" — never "we can read its mind".
- **Score: 9/10** — best evidence at frontier scale; pairs with item 3.

### 8. Your brain is a prediction machine too — demoted to one sentence, with a defended footnote
- **[corrected after red team: demoted; but the evidence base is stronger than the review assumed — see footnote]**
- **On-camera version (one line, via Hinton):** "Any system that handles uncertainty is a statistical model — including your brain. 'It's just statistics' isn't a criticism; the question is what structure the statistics built." Optional half-sentence: "and when neuroscientists record from human brains listening to speech, they find the brain predicting the next word too."
- **Defensive footnote (only if challenged):** The red team's attack was "n=9, ECoG, theory not consensus". Schrimpf et al., PNAS 2021: models that predict the next word best also predict brain activity best. Predictive processing (Clark 2013; Friston 2010) remains a theory, not consensus — say "a leading theory".
- **Links:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-022-01026-4 ; Caucheteux 2023 https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-022-01516-2 ; Goldstein 2025 https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02105-9 ; Schrimpf 2021 https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2105646118 ; Clark 2013 https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12000477 ; Friston 2010 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2787
- **Weaknesses:** Brains are embodied, multimodal, learn from far less data; Karpathy and Sutskever both stress the models are *not* like animals. Never say "LLMs work like brains".
- **Score: 6/10** [corrected after red team: 7→6; one line on camera, the rest is armour].

## B. "It's not just autocomplete anymore" — and what it has actually done

### 9. Reinforcement learning turned the predictor into a problem-solver
- **Core claim:** Since late 2024 the frontier models are not trained only to predict text. [corrected after red team: use Karpathy's textbook analogy] Pretraining is reading the textbook; supervised fine-tuning is studying worked examples; reinforcement learning is doing thousands of exercises with an answer key. Since 2024 the frontier models spend a large share of training doing exercises — maths and code with checkable answers — and they discovered on their own to write "wait, let me reconsider" and backtrack. Nobody programmed that. DeepSeek's *Nature* paper (Sept 2025) showed it emerging from pure RL with no human demonstrations. The models can also spend more compute at answer time ("test-time compute") and run as agents that use tools for hours.
- **Why this matters for the objection:** The "autocomplete" picture describes 2020–2023 base models. The systems in the news in 2025–26 are predictor *plus* RL-trained reasoner *plus* agent scaffold. Arguing about autocomplete is arguing about the wrong object.
- **Best quotes:** DeepSeek-R1 (Nature 2025): reasoning "can be incentivized through pure reinforcement learning, facilitating the emergent development of advanced reasoning patterns such as self-reflection, verification and dynamic strategy adaptation" (the "aha moment"). Karpathy (Dwarkesh, 17 Oct 2025): "Reinforcement learning is sucking supervision through a straw... you get one scalar at the end — pass or fail — and you broadcast that scalar to every token along the way."
- **Links:** Nature https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09422-z ; arXiv https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948 ; explainer https://huggingface.co/blog/NormalUhr/deepseek-r1-explained ; Karpathy "Deep Dive into LLMs like ChatGPT" (Feb 2025, textbook analogy) [verify URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xTGNNLPyMI ]; Dwarkesh–Karpathy https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/andrej-karpathy ; o1 (Sept 2024) and o3 (Dec 2024; 87.5% on ARC-AGI-1 at high compute) [verify URLs]
- **Strengths for a lay audience:** "Trained like a student doing exercises with an answer key, not like a parrot" is easy to grasp and explains why the jump happened in 2024–25.
- **Weaknesses / skeptic:** Apple's "The Illusion of Thinking" (June 2025) showed reasoning models collapsing on hard puzzle instances. Handle it honestly: some River Crossing instances in that paper were mathematically unsolvable and the Tower of Hanoi failures were partly output-length limits — but the limit it points at is real. [corrected after red team: citation] Rebuttal: A. Lawsen, "Comment on The Illusion of Thinking" (arXiv 2506.09250, June 2025) https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09250 ; a second, independent rebuttal: "Rethinking the Illusion of Thinking" (arXiv 2507.01231) https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01231v1 ; commentary https://www.seangoedecke.com/illusion-of-thinking/ . Also: RL on verifiable rewards is narrow (maths/code) — which is exactly what explains the jaggedness (item 19).
- **Caveats:** Don't claim RL "solved reasoning". Thinking traces are not guaranteed faithful (item 7).
- **Score: 9/10** — necessary; without it the whole debate is stale.

### 10. Gold medals in a competition nobody trained for
- **Core claim:** [corrected after red team: numbers] In July 2025 both Google DeepMind and OpenAI reported gold-medal scores (35/42, five of six problems) on the International Mathematical Olympiad, the hardest maths competition for pre-university students. IMO 2025: 630 contestants, 72 golds at a cutoff of 35, only **5** perfect scores (verified at imo-official.org; my earlier "67 golds / 26 perfect" was wrong). DeepMind's result was officially graded and certified by IMO coordinators; the model worked end-to-end in natural language within the 4.5-hour limit, whereas a year earlier its predecessor needed humans to translate problems into formal code and took days. In July 2026 (Shanghai, 15–16 July) **two** AI systems scored a perfect 42/42 under the IMO's official grading — Huawei's "Celia" and Xiaohongshu/RedNote's "dots-note-3.0" — alongside 7 of 666 human contestants. (Four other "42/42" claims for Western models that month came from a venture capitalist's self-administered test graded by AI agents; do not count them.) Two months after the 2025 IMO, at the ICPC World Finals (top university programming contest), OpenAI's system solved 12 of 12 problems; no human team solved more than 11, and one problem was solved by no human team at all.
- **Best quotes:** IMO President Gregor Dolinar (2025): "We can confirm that Google DeepMind has reached the much-desired milestone, earning 35 out of a possible 42 points — a gold medal score." DeepMind: the model "operated end-to-end in natural language... all within the 4.5-hour competition time limit."
- **Links:** DeepMind IMO blog (21 Jul 2025): https://deepmind.google/blog/advanced-version-of-gemini-with-deep-think-officially-achieves-gold-medal-standard-at-the-international-mathematical-olympiad/ ; IMO 2025 statistics: https://www.imo-official.org/editions/2025/ ; IMO 2026 perfect scores: https://www.scmp.com/tech/article/3361482/worlds-first-ai-model-earn-perfect-score-maths-olympiad-comes-chinas-rednote ; official-vs-self-administered breakdown: https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/imo-2026-perfect-scores-ai-benchmark-saturation ; ICPC 2025: https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-achieves-gold-medal-level-at-the-international-collegiate-programming-contest-world-finals/ , https://www.techrepublic.com/article/openai-deepmind-icpc-2025-results/
- **Anti-memorisation evidence [added after red team, missing argument #8]:** "It memorised the test" is testable. GSM1k (Zhang et al., Scale AI, May 2024, arXiv 2405.00332) wrote 1,250 fresh grade-school problems matching a famous benchmark: some model families dropped up to 8% (memorisation), but "many models, especially those on the frontier, show minimal signs of overfitting." And Olympiad problems are new every year. https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00332
- **Strengths for a lay audience:** Olympiad gold is a status everyone understands; "new problems every year" defeats "it memorised the answers".
- **Weaknesses / skeptic:** "Answer-key problems are exactly what RL is good at — puzzle-solving, not research"; labs used far more compute than a student; OpenAI's 2025 result was self-graded; the 2026 perfect scores came from Chinese labs (some viewers will discount). Rebuttal: narrow-but-superhuman is precisely the profile that matters for risk (item 19), and item 11 shows *open* problems falling too.
- **Caveats:** Say "gold-medal *level* score" for 2025 and "officially graded" only for DeepMind 2025 and the two 2026 systems. Do not say "five students scored higher" — five scored a *perfect* 42; how many scored 36–41 is not established here [verify if you want that line].
- **Score: 8/10** — cleanest capability headline; now with 2026's perfect scores.

### 11. New mathematics — with an honest scandal attached
- **Core claim, in three beats (hype → verified → claim) [corrected after red team]:**
  1. *The scandal.* In October 2025 an OpenAI executive tweeted that GPT-5 had "found solutions to 10 previously unsolved Erdős problems". It had found *existing* papers. Thomas Bloom (who runs erdosproblems.com): "a dramatic misrepresentation." Demis Hassabis: "embarrassing." The tweet was deleted.
  2. *The real thing.* On 20 May 2026 OpenAI's model produced a counterexample disproving Paul Erdős's 1946 unit-distance conjecture. Nine mathematicians — Noga Alon, Thomas Bloom, Tim Gowers (Fields Medal), Daniel Litt, Will Sawin, Arul Shankar, Jacob Tsimerman, Victor Wang and Melanie Matchett Wood — published "a short, digested, human-verified version" (arXiv 2605.20695). Gowers, about that paper: "if a human had written the paper and submitted it to the Annals of Mathematics and I had been asked for a quick opinion, I would have recommended acceptance without any hesitation." Noga Alon has stopped working on Erdős problems: "Once AI started to solve them, there is no point anymore." Separately, in April 2026 Liam Price, 23, with no research training, typed Erdős problem #1196 into ChatGPT (GPT-5.4 Pro) and got a proof — Tao's wiki lists it as a full solution "stronger than existing literature"; Terence Tao called it "a meaningful contribution to the anatomy of integers that goes well beyond the solution of this particular Erdős problem" and co-authored the follow-up paper (May 2026).
  3. *The claim.* On 8 Sept 2026 OpenAI announced that "an internal multiagent system" — around 10,000 agents running 88 hours from 1 Sept, ~2.7 million messages, ~130 billion tokens — had produced a 166-page paper plus a Lean formalisation claiming finite-time blow-up for the Navier–Stokes equations, a Millennium Prize problem. It is **unverified**: the Clay Institute (11 Sept) requires peer-reviewed publication and lists the problem as active; there is no confirmed match between the Lean statement and Clay's formulation; and there is a priority dispute with Tristan Buckmaster (NYU) and Levent Alpöge, who announced a related Euler result hours earlier and allege their Codex usage leaked (OpenAI denies). Tao: "a remarkable achievement" — and, in the same breath, a criticism of doing mathematics "by press releases or social media posts". **Only ever say: "claims to have… under review, disputed on credit."**
- **The honest tally [corrected after red team: replaced "15–20 problems" with Tao's own categories]:** Terence Tao's wiki of AI contributions lists roughly 50 entries in the "AI standalone" category (outcomes ranging from full solutions to incorrect work), ~26 "AI alongside literature", ~40 "AI building on literature", 150+ human–AI collaborations — and carries explicit disclaimers: it "is not a benchmark"; "absence of past progress may reflect obscurity rather than difficulty"; "a solution to an Erdős problem does not automatically qualify as publishable paper". Problem #397, sometimes cited as "solved in minutes", turned out to match a 2012 China TST competition problem — don't use it. Use #728 (6 Jan 2026, Aristotle + GPT-5.2 Pro, Lean-formalised, building on Pomerance 2014), #1196 and the unit-distance disproof. ### 22. Move 37 and AlphaZero: it is not capped by human data [added after red team, missing arguments #1–2]
- **Core claim:** "It can only remix what humans wrote" has a ten-year-old, undisputed counterexample. In March 2016, in game 2 against Lee Sedol, AlphaGo played a move (Move 37) that commentators called a mistake; AlphaGo itself estimated a human would play it about 1 time in 10,000; it won the game, and Go players now study it. A year later AlphaZero learned chess from the rules alone — no human games at all — by playing itself, and within hours beat the strongest existing engine. The lesson: reinforcement learning against a checkable outcome can go *past* the human data, not just imitate it. That is the same recipe now being applied to language models on maths and code (item 9), which is why the Olympiad results (item 10) and new theorems (item 11) should not surprise anyone.
- **Best quotes:** Lee Sedol on Move 37: "I thought AlphaGo was based on probability calculation and that it was merely a machine. But when I saw this move, I changed my mind. Surely, AlphaGo is creative." [verify wording; widely reported from the 2017 AlphaGo documentary]
- **Links:** Wired, "In Two Moves, AlphaGo and Lee Sedol Redefined the Future" (Mar 2016): https://www.wired.com/2016/03/two-moves-alphago-lee-sedol-redefined-future/ [verify URL]; Silver et al., "A general reinforcement learning algorithm that masters chess, shogi, and Go through self-play", Science 362, 1140 (Dec 2018): https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar6404 ; AlphaGo documentary (2017, free on YouTube).
- **Strengths:** Universally known, ten years old, no lab spin to discount; kills "capped by its training data" without touching philosophy.
- **Weaknesses / skeptic:** Games are closed worlds with perfect answer keys; language models are a different technology. Rebuttal: exactly — and the domains where LLMs now exceed humans (maths, code, exploits) are the ones with answer keys (item 19). Don't claim AlphaZero is an LLM.
- **Caveats:** AlphaZero's "4 hours" figure is from the 2017 preprint; the Science paper reports the full training. Say "within hours".
- **Score: 8/10** — the cheapest, most durable answer to "it only remixes".

### 23. It passed the Turing test — and why that doesn't settle anything [added after red team, missing argument #3]
- **Core claim:** Laypeople know the Turing test. In 2025 it was passed in its strict three-party form: judges chatted for five minutes with a human and a machine at the same time and had to pick the human. GPT-4.5, told to adopt a persona, was picked as the human **73%** of the time — more often than the actual human. Then apply item 15: Turing himself said whether machines "think" is "too meaningless to deserve discussion"; the test measures human-likeness, not competence, and competence is the risk question.
- **Evidence:** Jones & Bergen (UC San Diego), "Large Language Models Pass the Turing Test", arXiv 2503.23674 (31 Mar 2025): GPT-4.5 with persona prompt 73%; LLaMa-3.1-405B 56% (chance); GPT-4o without persona 21%; ELIZA 23%. https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23674
- **Strengths:** Instantly recognisable milestone; shows "it can't fool anyone" is over.
- **Weaknesses / skeptic:** Five-minute chats; needed a persona prompt; judges were not experts; "passing" a chat test proves human-likeness, not intelligence. All correct — which is the point of pairing it with item 15.
- **Score: 6/10** — hook, not evidence.
